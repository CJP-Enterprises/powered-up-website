# Where to Put an EV Charger in an Easton MA Garage

The best spot for your charger is the wall closest to your panel that still lets the cord reach your car's charge port without stretching. That one decision drives how clean the install looks and how much wire I have to run. Everything else follows from where you park and where your panel sits.

## Which Wall Should the Charger Go On?

Start with where your car actually sits when it's parked. Most charge ports are on the front left, front right, or rear of the vehicle, so I like to know the car before I pick a wall. If you back into the garage, that flips everything.

The wall you want is the one that keeps the cord off the floor and out of the walking path. A charger buried behind a parked car, forcing you to drag the cord across the hood, is a daily annoyance you'll regret.

Then there's the panel. In a lot of Easton homes, especially the older colonials off Depot Street and the split-levels out toward Furnace Village, the panel lives in the basement or on the far side of the garage. The farther the charger is from that panel, the more wire and effort it takes to connect them.

I try to land the charger on a wall that balances two things: easy for you to reach with the car, and a sane path back to the panel for me. Sometimes those line up perfectly. Sometimes we compromise, and I'll tell you exactly what each option costs you in convenience.

## Hardwired or Plug-In?

You've got two ways to mount a charger. It can be hardwired straight into the circuit, or it can plug into an outlet I install on the wall.

Plug-in gives you flexibility. If you move or swap the unit, you unplug it. Hardwired tends to look cleaner and is often the move for outdoor-rated setups or units mounted where a receptacle would be awkward.

Which one makes sense depends on the charger you bought, where it's going, and how exposed the spot is. I'll walk through both with you on site instead of guessing before I see the wall.

## What About Running the Wire?

This is the part most people don't think about, and it's where a good install separates itself from a sloppy one.

If your panel is in the garage on the same wall, the run is short and simple. If it's in a finished basement under the garage, I'm fishing wire through framing, drilling through the sill, and coming up where you want the charger. That takes more time and more care to keep it neat.

Here's what I look at when I walk the space:

- Where the panel is and whether it has room for another breaker
- Whether the run can go through unfinished space or has to cross finished ceilings and walls
- Attic access, if the cleanest path is up and over
- Whether any of the run is exposed to the outdoors or an unheated area

Winters here matter more than people think. An unheated detached garage off Bay Road sees real cold, and I account for that in how I route and protect the wire. The stuff on the exposed exterior wall gets treated differently than the stuff tucked inside conditioned space.

## Does My Panel Even Have Room?

A charger pulls a serious load, and your panel has to have the capacity and a free spot to feed it. This is the question that surprises people.

If your panel is already packed, or it's an older service that's near its limit, adding a charger might mean freeing up space or upgrading the panel first. I check this before I ever talk about mounting locations, because there's no point picking a pretty spot if the panel can't feed it.

When I look at your panel, I'm checking the amperage of the service, how loaded it already is, and whether there's a clean way to add the new circuit. If it needs work first, I'll tell you straight and we can talk about a [panel upgrade](/services/panel-upgrades) as part of the plan.

I'd rather have that conversation up front than halfway through the job.

## How I Keep the Install Clean

The difference between a charger that looks factory and one that looks like a weekend project is in the details. I mount the unit level, at a height that's comfortable to reach and lets the cord hang and hook without dragging.

Exposed conduit, when it's needed, gets run in straight lines with proper bends, not zigzagged across the wall. Where the wire enters the panel, it's landed clean and labeled so the next person who opens that panel knows exactly what they're looking at.

Small things add up. A charger you use every single day should feel like it was always meant to be there.
