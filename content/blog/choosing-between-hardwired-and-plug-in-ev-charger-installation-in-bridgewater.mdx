# Choosing Between Hardwired and Plug-In EV Charger Installation in Bridgewater

If you just bought an EV and you're staring at your garage wall wondering how the charger actually attaches, here's the short version: you've got two ways to go, hardwired or plug-in, and both work fine when the install is done right. The right choice depends on your charger, your panel, and where the unit is going to live.

Let me walk you through how I think about it so you can make the call before I show up.

## What's the actual difference?

A plug-in charger comes with a cord and a plug on the end. I install a dedicated outlet on the wall, usually a 240-volt receptacle, and you plug the charger in like a big appliance. The charger hangs on a bracket next to it.

A hardwired charger skips the plug entirely. The wire runs straight from the circuit into the back of the unit, with no outlet in between. It's a permanent connection.

That's the whole concept. Everything else is tradeoffs around convenience, location, and what your equipment can handle.

## When does plug-in make more sense?

Plug-in is the easy-swap option. If your charger ever fails or you upgrade to a newer model, you unplug the old one and plug in the new one. No electrician needed for the swap itself.

It's also handy if you think you might move the charger, or take it with you if you sell the house. A lot of folks like knowing they're not leaving a few hundred dollars of hardware bolted to the wall.

The catch is the outlet itself. A 240-volt receptacle carrying that kind of load needs to be the right type and installed properly, because a loose or undersized plug connection is where heat problems start. I use quality receptacles rated for the job, not whatever's cheapest on the shelf.

## When does hardwired make more sense?

Hardwired tends to be the better pick for an outdoor install or a damp garage. Fewer connection points means fewer spots for moisture to get in, which matters out here in Southeastern Massachusetts where a Bridgewater garage can swing from humid in August to below freezing in January.

Some chargers also only run at their full output when they're hardwired. If you've got a higher-amperage unit and you want every bit of charging speed it offers, hardwiring can be the way to get there. Check your charger's documentation, or send me the model and I'll tell you.

Hardwired also looks cleaner. No outlet, no cord loop, just the unit and the conduit. If the charger's going somewhere visible, that's worth something.

## What actually drives the decision on my end?

When I'm weighing hardwired vs plug in EV charger setups for a home in Bridgewater, I'm looking at a handful of things on site before I commit to either:

- Your charger make and model, and whether the manufacturer calls for hardwiring to hit full output
- Whether the unit is going inside the garage or outside on an exterior wall
- The distance from your electrical panel to the charger location, which drives the wire run
- What your panel can support, and whether you've got room for the new circuit
- Moisture and temperature where the charger will live

That last one matters more than people expect. An exterior wall facing the weather is a different job than a spot tucked inside a dry garage. The mounting location and the wiring approach feed into each other, which is why I usually talk both through at the same time. If you're still deciding where the unit goes, I wrote up the [garage versus outdoor mounting tradeoffs](/blog/choosing-between-garage-and-outdoor-ev-charger-mounting-in-bridgewater) in a separate post.

## Does my panel matter for either option?

Yes, and it's the part people skip. Both a plug-in outlet and a hardwired charger need their own dedicated circuit running back to your panel. If your panel is already full, or it's an older service that's maxed out, we deal with that first.

This comes up a lot in some of the older homes around Bridgewater and the surrounding towns. An addition got put on years ago, the garage got finished, and now there's just no room left in the box. Sometimes a charger install turns into a conversation about a [panel upgrade](/services/panel-upgrades) before anything else happens.

I'd rather find that out on the first visit than surprise you with it halfway through. When I come look at the job, I check the panel, the proposed charger spot, and the run between them, then I tell you straight what the install actually needs.

## How do I decide which one you should get?

Honestly, I ask what you care about. If you want flexibility and easy future swaps, plug-in usually wins. If you want the cleanest look, the most weather resistance, or the full speed out of a bigger charger, I lean hardwired.

There's no universally right answer, and anybody telling you otherwise over the phone hasn't seen your garage. I'd rather look at it, understand how you'll use it, and match the install to that.
