# Troubleshooting Flickering Lights When Heavy Loads Run in Easton

If your lights dip or flicker the moment your AC compressor, well pump, or electric dryer kicks on, that's your house telling you something. Sometimes it's harmless. Sometimes it's a loose connection getting hot behind a panel cover, and that one you don't want to ignore.

Here's how I sort out which is which.

## Why do my lights flicker when something big turns on?

Big motor loads pull a surge of current the instant they start. Your AC condenser, a well pump, a sump pump, even a microwave all grab extra power for a split second before they settle. That momentary draw causes a small voltage dip, and your lights show it.

A tiny, brief flicker when the central air starts on a hot July afternoon can be normal. Everything on that circuit shares the same supply, and motors are greedy at startup.

What's not normal is a flicker that's getting worse, that happens across the whole house, or that comes with a buzzing sound, a warm outlet, or a faint burning smell. That's the difference between a quirk and a problem.

## What actually causes the flickering?

Most of what I find in older Easton homes comes down to a handful of things. A lot of the housing stock around town, especially near the Village and the older streets off Washington Street, has decades of wear on connections that were fine the day they were made.

Here's what I check for:

- **Loose or backstabbed connections.** Wires pushed into the quick-connect holes on the back of outlets and switches loosen over time. A loose connection has resistance, resistance makes heat, and heat makes flicker.
- **A loose neutral.** This is the one I take seriously. A bad neutral, whether at an outlet, in a junction box, or at the panel, can make lights on one side of the house brighten while the other side dims. It can damage electronics and it can get hot.
- **An overloaded circuit.** Too many heavy loads sharing one branch circuit. The lights and the dryer shouldn't be fighting over the same wire.
- **A tired panel or breaker.** Worn breakers and corroded panel connections cause voltage drop under load.
- **Service or utility side issues.** Sometimes the dip starts before your meter. If it's coming from the utility's drop or the connection at your weatherhead, that changes who fixes it.

## How do I track it down?

I start by asking what's running when it flickers. If it's always the same appliance, that narrows the circuit right away. If the whole house flickers at once, I'm thinking about the neutral or the service feed, not a single outlet.

Then I pull the panel cover and look. I'm checking for discoloration, loose lugs, and connections that feel warm. I'll put a meter on it and watch the voltage while the heavy load cycles, so I can actually see the dip and where it's happening instead of guessing.

From there I work outward. I'll open suspect outlets and switches, especially the ones in line before the fixtures that flicker, because the problem is usually upstream of where you see it. Backstabbed devices get moved to the screw terminals. Loose splices get redone properly.

This kind of electrical troubleshooting is detective work. The flicker is the symptom, and the whole point is finding the one bad connection in a hundred feet of wire before it turns into something worse.

## Is flickering actually dangerous?

It can be. The connections that cause flicker are the same connections that heat up, and heat inside a wall is how fires start. Not every flicker is an emergency, but a flicker paired with heat, a smell, or a buzzing sound should get looked at soon.

A failing neutral is the one I'd move on quickly. When the neutral gets weak, voltage on your circuits can swing high and low. That's rough on anything with a motor or a circuit board, so it can quietly kill electronics while it's at it.

If you've got scorch marks around an outlet, an outlet or switch plate that's warm to the touch, or breakers that trip along with the flicker, stop using that circuit and get it checked.

## What about my panel and the EV charger I'm adding?

A lot of flicker calls I take in Easton and the surrounding towns come up right when someone's adding a big new load. Around here that's often an EV charger. A Level 2 charger is a serious continuous draw, and if your panel was already tight, adding it can turn a faint flicker into an obvious one.

If your lights dip hard when the charger or another heavy load runs, it's worth having the panel evaluated for capacity and condition at the same time I chase the flicker. Sometimes the fix is a bad connection. Sometimes the real answer is that the panel is maxed out and needs attention before you add anything else.
