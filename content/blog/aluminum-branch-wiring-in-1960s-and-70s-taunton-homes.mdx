# Aluminum Branch Wiring in 1960s and 70s Taunton Homes

If your house went up in Taunton somewhere between the mid 1960s and the late 1970s, there's a real chance the branch circuits feeding your outlets and switches are aluminum instead of copper. That was common building practice for a stretch of years, and it's not something to panic over, but it is something to have looked at. The connections are where the trouble shows up, not the wire itself sitting in the wall.

## How do I know if my house has aluminum wiring?

The clearest tell is the wire itself. Aluminum branch wiring has a dull silver color where copper is that familiar reddish tone. Sometimes you can see it at an outlet or in the panel, and older cable jackets will occasionally have "AL" or "ALUMINUM" printed right on them.

A lot of homes in this era around Taunton, out toward Whittenton and the older neighborhoods off Bay Street, went up fast during that building stretch. That's the window I pay attention to when someone calls.

I don't want you pulling outlets apart to go looking. If you're not sure, that's exactly the kind of thing I check when I come out. It takes me a few minutes at the panel and a couple of receptacles to get a solid read on what you've got.

## Why is aluminum wiring a concern?

Aluminum and copper behave differently. Aluminum expands and contracts more with heat, and over decades of a circuit warming up and cooling down, connections can loosen. It also oxidizes, and that oxide layer adds resistance right where the wire lands on a screw terminal.

Loose, high-resistance connections are where heat builds. The wire in the wall is usually fine. It's the terminations at outlets, switches, and splices that need attention.

That's why the fix is almost never "rewire the whole house." Most of the time it's about making the connections right at each device.

## What are the warning signs I should watch for?

You don't need to be an electrician to notice these. Call me if you run into any of them:

- Outlet or switch cover plates that feel warm to the touch
- A faint burning or fishy plastic smell near a receptacle
- Outlets or switches that have stopped working, or work only sometimes
- Flickering lights that aren't tied to one bad bulb
- Any scorch marks or discoloration around a plate

Any one of these is worth a look. More than one on the same circuit, and I'd want to get out there sooner rather than later.

## How do you actually fix it?

The industry-accepted approach is to make a proper connection between the existing aluminum and a short piece of copper at each device, using connectors rated for that job, and then land the copper on the outlet or switch. Done right, you get a solid, low-resistance termination that isn't fighting the metal.

There's also the option of swapping in devices specifically rated to accept aluminum directly. Which route makes sense depends on what I find in your boxes, how much slack the original installer left, and the condition of the wire ends.

The one thing I won't do is a quick "wire nut and move on." The connection method matters more than almost anything else here, and using the wrong connector on aluminum is how these problems start in the first place.
