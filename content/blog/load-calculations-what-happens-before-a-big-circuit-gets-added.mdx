# Load Calculations: What Happens Before a Big Circuit Gets Added

Before I add a heavy circuit to your house, an EV charger, a new electric range, a hot tub, I figure out whether your existing panel can actually carry it. That's the load calculation. It's the math that tells me if we're good to go on your current service or if the panel needs to come first.

Most people call me wanting the charger. The load calc is the part nobody asks about, and it's the part that decides whether the whole job is simple or not.

## What is a load calculation, really?

It's an accounting of everything in your house that draws power, measured against what your electrical service can supply. Your panel has a rating. The question is how much of that rating is already spoken for before we add one more big thing.

I look at the big draws first. Heat, air conditioning, water heater if it's electric, range, dryer, and anything else that pulls serious current. Then the general lighting and receptacle load based on the size of the house.

When I add up what you're running and compare it to the capacity of your service, I can see how much headroom is left. An EV charger is a big continuous load, so it eats into that headroom fast. That's why a charger install so often turns into a panel conversation.

## Why does an EV charger trigger this?

A car charger isn't like plugging in a toaster. It pulls a steady, heavy load for hours at a stretch, usually overnight. Continuous loads get treated differently in the math because the circuit has to hold up under that draw the whole time, not just a quick spike.

A lot of homes around Bridgewater were wired for a different era. If you're in an older place off Pleasant Street or out toward the lakes, your service may have been sized before anyone imagined charging a car in the garage. It was plenty for how the house was used then.

Add in that around here people lean hard on electric heat and AC through our swing from humid summers to cold winters, and the existing service can already be working harder than you'd think. That's exactly what an electrical load calculation in Bridgewater MA is meant to catch before we commit to a new circuit.

## How do I actually do it on your house?

I come out and look. I open the panel and see what's in there, what's already on each circuit, and how the service comes in from the meter.

Here's what I'm checking:

- The rating of your existing service and panel
- What major appliances you have and how they're powered
- How many spare breaker spaces are left, if any
- The condition of the panel and the wiring feeding it
- Where the new circuit has to land and how far the wire has to run

From there I run the numbers. If there's room, we add the circuit and you're set. If the math says the service is maxed, I'll tell you straight that we're looking at a [panel upgrade](/services/panel-upgrades) before the new load goes in.

I'd rather have that conversation up front than put you in a panel that's stretched too thin.

## What if my panel can't handle it?

This happens, and it isn't a disaster. It just changes the scope of the work. A panel upgrade gives you more capacity and clean breaker space, which sets you up not just for the charger but for whatever comes next.

Think about where you're headed. If the EV charger is step one and you're eyeing an electric range or a heat pump down the road, it's worth sizing for that now instead of doing this twice.

The upgrade also involves the utility and, depending on your address, the town. I coordinate that side of it. What the permitting and inspection steps look like for your specific property is something I confirm as part of the job, so you're not chasing paperwork on your own.

## What drives the cost of all this?

The honest answer is that it depends on what I find. A straightforward add with plenty of panel headroom is one thing. A full service upgrade with a long wire run and utility coordination is another.

The things that move the number are the condition and rating of your current panel, how much the service has to grow, where the new circuit lands, and how far the wire travels to get there. Those vary enough house to house that quoting you blind would just be a guess, and a guess doesn't help either of us.

Once I've walked your panel and run the load calc, I can give you a real number for your house. Not a range I pulled out of the air.
