# Sizing a Whole-House Generator for Your Taunton MA Home

Sizing a generator right comes down to one thing: what you actually need running when the power drops. Not the sticker on the box, not what your neighbor bought. I start by walking your house and adding up the loads you can't live without, then match a unit to that.

Get this wrong and you either overspend on a unit that's bigger than your house needs, or you buy one that trips and stalls the first time the well pump and the furnace kick on together. Here's how I think through it.

## What does "whole-house" actually mean for your home?

"Whole-house" gets thrown around loosely. For some folks it means every circuit runs like nothing happened. For others it means the essentials: heat, fridge, well pump, a few outlets, and enough lighting to move around safely.

The difference between those two changes the size of the unit and the cost of the install. A true whole-house setup usually pairs a larger standby generator with an automatic transfer switch that manages the loads. A smaller essentials setup can run off a more modest unit with a subpanel feeding just the circuits that matter.

Neither one is wrong. It depends on how you live and what you're willing to spend. I'll lay out both when I'm at your house so you can see the tradeoff.

## What drives the size you need?

The number that matters is your peak load, meaning the most power your house draws at once, including the surge when motors start up. A well pump, a furnace blower, and a central AC compressor all pull a big spike the instant they turn on.

Here's what I look at when I'm sizing a whole house generator in Taunton MA:

- **Heat.** Around here that's the big one. If you've got a gas or oil furnace, the blower motor and controls need power. Electric heat is a much heavier load and changes the math fast.
- **Well and septic pumps.** Plenty of homes outside downtown Taunton, out toward the Raynham and Bridgewater lines, run on private wells. No power means no water, so the pump usually makes the must-run list.
- **Refrigeration and cooking.** Fridge, freezer, and whether you want the range and oven working.
- **AC.** Summer outages during a thunderstorm knock power out just when you want the cooling running.
- **Well-startup surge.** This is the one people forget. A generator has to swallow the motor surge, not just the running load.

I add these up the way they actually behave, not just the labels on the breakers. That's the part a load calculator on a website can't do for you.

## Natural gas or propane in this area?

Fuel decides a lot. Standby generators here typically run on natural gas or propane. If you're on a gas main, tying into it is often the simplest path. Out in the more rural stretches of Easton and the outer parts of town where there's no gas service, propane off a tank is the usual answer.

Propane means sizing a tank big enough to run through a multi-day outage, which we do get after nor'easters and the occasional ice storm that comes through southeastern Massachusetts. I'll talk through fuel availability at your address because it affects both the generator you pick and where it gets placed.

Placement matters too. The unit needs clearance, a spot that stays clear of snow drifts, and a run to the transfer switch that makes sense. I sort that out on site.

## Do I need a permit and an inspection?

Plan on this being permitted, inspected electrical work, and there's a gas side to it too if you're on natural gas or propane. What exactly is required for your specific address and setup is a question for the Taunton building and wiring inspection offices, and they can confirm it before you commit to anything.

I handle the electrical side of the install and the transfer switch, which is the part that has to be done right so your generator never backfeeds the grid. That's not a DIY corner to cut. If you want to see the kind of panel and service work that ties into a generator install, take a look at my [panel upgrade and service work](/services/panel-upgrades), since an older panel sometimes needs attention before a generator makes sense.

## What about my existing panel?

This is where a lot of generator jobs actually start. If your service panel is undersized, full, or an older model that's tough to add to, we deal with that first. The transfer switch and the generator circuits have to land somewhere, and a crowded or outdated panel can turn a clean install into a bigger project.

I'd rather tell you that up front than surprise you. When I walk the house I'll open the panel, check the service size, and let you know if it's ready or if it needs work first.

## What will it cost?

The honest answer is I can't give you a real number without seeing the house. What moves the price is the size of the unit, the fuel type and tank if propane, how far the generator sits from the panel, whether your existing service can handle it, and how many circuits you want covered.

Those swing the total enough that quoting you sight unseen would just be a guess, and a bad guess helps nobody. I'll give you a firm number once I've walked the property and we've settled on what you want running during an outage.
